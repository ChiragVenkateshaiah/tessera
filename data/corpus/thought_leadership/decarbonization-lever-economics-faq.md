## Twelve Questions

Across decarbonization work with heavy manufacturers, the same questions come up in board discussions with striking consistency — and they are usually answered less directly than they deserve. Short answers below; the fuller argument sits in our piece on decarbonization in heavy manufacturing.

### Should we set an internal carbon price?

Yes, if you will actually let it change decisions. An internal carbon price that appears in the investment paper but never changes an outcome is worse than none, because it creates the appearance of discipline without the substance. Set it, apply it to every capital decision above threshold, and be prepared for it to reject projects that would otherwise pass.

### What level should it be set at?

High enough to change marginal decisions, and defensible against some external reference — a compliance market price, a forward carbon price curve, or an internally derived abatement cost. What matters more than the level is that it is uniform across the business. A price that varies by division becomes a negotiation rather than a constraint.

### Why do abatement cost curves mislead?

Because they present levers as independent when they are frequently sequential. A curve implies you can pick the cheapest measures in any order; in practice, many low-cost levers only become available after an enabling investment that appears expensive in isolation. Reading a curve left to right and committing to the cheap end first is how programmes stall at roughly a third of their target.

### Are the cheap levers really cheap?

The first tranche usually is — efficiency measures with genuinely attractive standalone returns. The problem is that they are also finite and often already partly captured. Programmes that treat the cheap tranche as representative of the whole curve consistently underestimate the capital required for the remainder by a wide margin.

### Should we buy offsets?

As a bridge for genuinely hard-to-abate residual emissions, defensibly. As a substitute for abatement in areas where abatement is available but inconvenient, no — and increasingly not in the eyes of the people scrutinising the claim. The practical test is whether you could publish the offset share of your reduction without discomfort.

### How should we treat Scope 3 in capital allocation?

Differently from Scope 1 and 2, because you do not control it. Scope 3 belongs in supplier strategy, product design, and commercial terms rather than in the capital plan. Attempting to buy Scope 3 reductions through your own capital programme is the most common category error we see, and it produces expensive projects with weak attribution.

### What does electrification actually cost us?

More than the equipment, and the gap is usually grid connection. Connection timelines and capacity constraints in many industrial regions now determine the sequencing of electrification programmes more than the equipment economics do. The connection query should precede the business case, not follow it.

### Is hydrogen a real option yet?

For specific high-temperature and chemical-feedstock applications, it is becoming a credible medium-term option. For applications where electrification is technically viable, the honest comparison usually favours electrification on both cost and delivery risk. The useful board question is not "should we do hydrogen" but "which of our processes have no electrification path," because that is a much shorter list than it is usually assumed to be.

### How do we handle assets with remaining useful life?

By separating the emissions decision from the accounting one. Early retirement of a serviceable asset carries a real write-down, and pretending otherwise damages the programme's credibility with finance. Model it explicitly, and expect that in some cases the right answer is to run the asset to end of life while committing to the replacement decision now.

### What return should we require?

The same return as any other capital, adjusted by the internal carbon price. Creating a separate, softer hurdle for decarbonization projects feels supportive and is corrosive — it signals that these investments cannot compete on their merits and makes them the first casualty of any capital squeeze.

### How much of this is regulation-driven versus value-driven?

Both, in a proportion that varies enormously by jurisdiction and sector, and the split matters because it determines reversibility. Regulation-driven investment is exposed to regulatory change; value-driven investment is not. Programmes that cannot articulate which of their commitments are which are poorly positioned for a policy shift in either direction.

### What is the most common reason programmes stall?

Sequencing, not ambition. Most stalled programmes we see captured the accessible efficiency measures, reported strong early progress, and then hit a step change in capital intensity that the original plan had not made visible. The programmes that sustain momentum are the ones that surfaced the full capital profile at the outset, including the uncomfortable middle, rather than letting an encouraging first year set expectations the second year could not meet.

## The Pattern Underneath

Ten of these twelve questions are capital allocation questions wearing sustainability clothing, and they are answered best with ordinary capital allocation discipline rather than with a parallel decision process. The organisations making genuine progress are conspicuously the ones that stopped treating decarbonization as a separate category of investment with its own rules, and started running it through the same scrutiny — the same hurdle rates, the same scenario testing, the same willingness to stop things — as everything else on the capital plan.
