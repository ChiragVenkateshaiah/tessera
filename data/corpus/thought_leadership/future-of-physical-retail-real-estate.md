## The Wrong Question Retailers Keep Asking

For a decade, the dominant retail real estate question has been some version of "how many stores should we close as e-commerce grows." It's an understandable question given the headline store-closure numbers that dominate retail news coverage, but it's increasingly the wrong question for retailers doing sophisticated omnichannel planning — because it treats the physical store purely as a sales-transaction channel competing against e-commerce, when for most retailers doing this well, the store's actual economic role has shifted to something the closure-count framing misses entirely.

## The Store as Fulfillment and Acquisition Infrastructure

Across the retail clients we've worked with on network and inventory optimization, a consistent pattern has emerged: stores are increasingly valuable not for what they sell directly across the register, but for the fulfillment infrastructure and customer acquisition function they provide to the broader omnichannel business. A store within a reasonable delivery radius that also serves as a ship-from-store fulfillment node, a pickup point, and a returns-processing location is generating economic value that doesn't show up cleanly in a same-store-sales metric built for a single-channel retail era.

This reframes the real estate question in a genuinely useful way. Instead of "is this store's direct sales performance strong enough to justify keeping it open," the better question is "what is this location's total contribution across direct sales, fulfillment enablement, and customer acquisition for the surrounding catchment area's omnichannel demand" — and retailers answering only the first question are making real estate decisions on an incomplete picture, closing locations that are quietly propping up e-commerce economics in their catchment area, or keeping open locations whose fulfillment role doesn't actually justify their real estate cost.

## Format Innovation Beyond "Bigger or Smaller"

The format conversation has also matured past a simple bigger-versus-smaller-footprint debate. What we're seeing work well across several retail categories:

**Right-sized formats built explicitly around a defined fulfillment and experience role**, rather than a generic smaller version of a full-format store — a format designed from the ground up to serve as a fulfillment-and-pickup-optimized location performs differently, and should look physically different, than a full-assortment flagship format, even if both are nominally "smaller."

**Experiential investment concentrated in fewer, more deliberate locations**, rather than spread evenly across the network — the store's role as a brand-building, experience-driving asset (rather than a transaction location) justifies real investment in a subset of flagship or high-visibility locations, while the broader network optimizes more heavily for fulfillment efficiency and cost.

**Format decisions increasingly tied to network optimization modeling**, not made store-by-store in isolation — a store's right format and size depends partly on its role within the broader fulfillment network, which means format strategy and network optimization (see the supply chain network optimization methodology) need to be solved together, not as sequential, disconnected exercises.

## The Real Estate Portfolio Discipline This Requires

None of this means physical retail real estate strategy gets easier — if anything, it gets more analytically demanding, because it now requires genuinely integrating real estate, fulfillment network, and customer acquisition economics into a single decision framework, rather than the historically more siloed approach where real estate decisions were made primarily on direct store sales performance. Retailers still running real estate portfolio decisions off a same-store-sales-and-lease-cost model alone are, in effect, using a single-channel decision tool for a multi-channel economic reality, and it shows up in decisions that look rational on the metric being measured and wrong on the metric that actually matters.

## What This Means Going Forward

The store count will likely keep declining in aggregate for many retail categories — that trend isn't reversing. But the more important strategic story for the next several years isn't the aggregate number; it's which retailers correctly identify and invest in the subset of their real estate portfolio that's earning its keep through a broader, more accurate view of total economic contribution, and which retailers are still cutting and keeping stores based on a metric that no longer captures what a store is actually worth to the business.
