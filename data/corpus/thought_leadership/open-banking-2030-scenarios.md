## Why Scenarios Rather Than a Forecast

Every payments forecast written in the last decade has been wrong in the same direction: right about the destination, wrong about the pace, and wrong in ways that made the intervening strategy decisions worse rather than better. The useful discipline is not a better point forecast. It is a small set of genuinely distinct end-states, each with the conditions that would have to hold for it to arrive and the observable signposts that would tell you early which one you are in.

> A forecast tells you what to plan for. A scenario set tells you what to watch for. Only one of those is reliably useful at a five-year horizon.

The four below are built around two axes that we think matter more than any others: whether the customer relationship consolidates or fragments, and whether regulation continues to push standardisation or fractures across jurisdictions. They are deliberately mutually exclusive; the real world will be a blend, but a blend is not a planning tool.

### Scenario One — Incumbent-Retained

**What happens.** Banks absorb open banking as a compliance obligation and successfully defend the primary customer relationship. Account-to-account payment volume grows, but predominantly within bank-owned propositions. Third-party providers occupy specific niches rather than the payment layer generally.

**What has to be true.** Incumbent execution has to be materially better than it was in the first wave of open banking. Banks have to make account-to-account payment as easy at the point of sale as a card is, and they have to do it before customer habit forms elsewhere. Regulation has to remain access-focused rather than actively promoting alternatives.

**Signposts to watch.**

1. Bank-owned payment initiation propositions reaching genuine point-of-sale ubiquity rather than remaining confined to bill payment
2. Third-party payment initiation volume growth decelerating rather than compounding
3. Merchant acquirers continuing to route through card rails by default even where alternatives exist

> The strongest argument for this scenario is also the least satisfying one: customer inertia in payments has repeatedly outlasted every prediction of its demise.

### Scenario Two — Aggregator-Dominated

**What happens.** A small number of aggregators own the interface, and banks become the balance-sheet and settlement layer beneath it. The customer's relationship is with the aggregator; the bank is infrastructure.

**What has to be true.** Aggregators have to solve trust at scale, which means either regulatory status equivalent to a bank's or a brand strong enough to substitute for it. Data portability has to work well enough that switching underlying providers is genuinely frictionless. And banks have to continue treating the interface layer as lower-value than the balance sheet.

**Signposts to watch.**

1. Aggregator-originated transaction share crossing the threshold where merchants design for it first
2. Banks accepting white-label distribution deals that concede the customer-facing relationship
3. Regulatory frameworks extending bank-equivalent obligations, and therefore bank-equivalent legitimacy, to non-bank interface providers

### Scenario Three — Fragmented by Regulation

**What happens.** Divergent regulatory regimes across major jurisdictions make cross-border propositions structurally expensive. Payments consolidate regionally rather than globally, and scale advantages accrue within blocs rather than across them.

**What has to be true.** Data localisation, differing consent frameworks, and incompatible technical standards have to persist rather than converge. This scenario does not require any actor to intend fragmentation; it only requires that no actor has sufficient incentive to bear the cost of harmonisation.

**Signposts to watch.**

1. Divergence rather than convergence in consent and authentication standards between major regimes
2. Global providers withdrawing from or de-prioritising specific jurisdictions on compliance-cost grounds
3. Regional payment schemes gaining share against global card networks within their own blocs

> Fragmentation is the scenario incumbents most consistently under-weight, because it is the only one where the winning strategy is regional depth rather than global scale — and global scale is the strategy most large institutions have already committed to.

### Scenario Four — Embedded and Invisible

**What happens.** Payment ceases to be a distinct customer moment. It is initiated inside commerce, logistics, and software workflows, and the visible brand belongs to the context rather than to the payment provider or the bank.

**What has to be true.** Embedded finance infrastructure has to become genuinely commoditised, so that any software vendor can offer payment without building it. Liability and dispute resolution have to be settled cleanly enough that non-financial firms will accept the exposure. And the economics have to work at the thin margins that invisibility implies.

**Signposts to watch.**

1. Software platforms outside financial services becoming material payment volume originators
2. Dispute and chargeback frameworks being extended to cover embedded contexts
3. Payment margin compressing to a level that only volume-scaled infrastructure providers can sustain

## What Incumbents Should Do Regardless

The value of the scenario set is not in picking one. It is in identifying the moves that are sensible across several — and there are more of those than the strategic debate usually acknowledges.

Investment in payment infrastructure that is genuinely reusable across interfaces pays off in three of the four scenarios and is not badly wrong in the fourth. Building the capability to be a good infrastructure provider — reliable, well-documented, economically priced — is a hedge against aggregator dominance rather than a concession to it. Regional depth is valuable in the fragmented scenario and merely adequate elsewhere, which makes it a reasonable insurance purchase rather than a bet.

What is genuinely scenario-dependent, and therefore what deserves the strategic attention, is how much to invest in owning the customer interface. That is the decision the signposts above should inform, and it is the one most institutions are currently making on conviction rather than on evidence. Treating it as a scenario-contingent decision — with pre-agreed triggers, in the way the scenario planning methodology describes — is a better discipline than continuing to argue about whose forecast is right.
