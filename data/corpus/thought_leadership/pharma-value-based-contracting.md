## A Decade of Talk, a Fraction of the Adoption

Value-based contracting — pharmaceutical pricing tied to real-world patient outcomes rather than a fixed price per unit regardless of clinical result — has been discussed as the inevitable future of drug pricing for the better part of a decade. Adoption has been real but far slower and narrower than the early enthusiasm suggested. Understanding why is more useful than another round of predicting imminent transformation, because the barriers are structural, not simply a matter of willingness, and they shape which manufacturers should actually pursue value-based contracts now versus wait.

## Why Adoption Has Lagged the Rhetoric

**Outcomes measurement infrastructure remains genuinely hard.** A value-based contract requires both parties to agree on what outcome is being measured, over what time horizon, using what data source — and healthcare data infrastructure, particularly the ability to track a specific patient's outcome longitudinally across care settings and link it back to a specific therapy, remains fragmented in most markets. Contracts that sound elegant in a term sheet frequently stall in implementation because neither party can actually operationalize the measurement cleanly.

**Rebate-driven list pricing dynamics complicate the economics.** In markets where net price is heavily shaped by rebates negotiated with intermediaries rather than list price alone, the economic logic of a value-based contract — price adjusts based on outcome — interacts awkwardly with an already-complex rebate structure, and the administrative complexity of layering outcome-based adjustment on top of existing rebate mechanics has been a genuine, underappreciated barrier.

**Payer risk appetite varies more than manufacturers initially assumed.** Not every payer is equally positioned or motivated to take on the administrative and financial complexity of an outcomes-based deal — payers with strong internal data infrastructure and a genuine population-health incentive structure have engaged meaningfully; others have treated value-based contracting pilots as a box-checking exercise without the operational investment to make them work at scale.

## Where It's Actually Working

The clearest successes share common characteristics worth naming explicitly. They tend to involve therapies with a clear, relatively short-time-horizon, objectively measurable outcome (a specific lab value, a readmission event, a clear treatment-response marker) rather than a long-horizon, harder-to-attribute outcome like overall survival in a chronic condition. They tend to involve payers with mature data infrastructure already in place, rather than requiring net-new measurement capability to be built specifically for the contract. And they tend to be structured with a genuinely two-sided incentive — the manufacturer benefits from demonstrating real-world value in a competitive category, and the payer benefits from meaningfully reduced financial risk on a high-cost therapy — rather than a one-sided arrangement dressed up in value-based language.

## What This Means for Manufacturer Pricing Strategy Now

The practical implication for pharma manufacturers evaluating whether to pursue value-based contracting for a given therapy: the decision should be driven by whether the specific therapy and market context match the pattern above, not by a general strategic belief that "value-based is the future and we should be doing it." Using the value-based pricing methodology's discipline — building a rigorous, buyer-credible value model before structuring any contract — matters even more in a value-based contracting context than in standard value-based list pricing, because the contract's success depends on both parties being able to actually measure and agree on the outcome that's supposed to drive the price adjustment.

For manufacturers in categories where measurement infrastructure and payer readiness aren't yet in place, the more productive near-term investment may be contributing to building that infrastructure — through real-world evidence generation, payer data partnerships — rather than attempting to force a value-based structure into a market context that isn't yet operationally ready to support one. The manufacturers we see getting real value from value-based contracting today are the ones who did that groundwork deliberately, years before signing the first contract, rather than treating outcomes measurement as something to figure out after the deal terms were agreed.

## The Longer Arc

None of this means the original thesis was wrong — value-based contracting likely does represent a genuine long-term direction for a meaningful share of pharmaceutical pricing, particularly as real-world data infrastructure matures across health systems. But the timeline has proven to be measured in the infrastructure-building pace of the broader healthcare data ecosystem, not the pace of any individual manufacturer's strategic ambition, and pricing strategy needs to be built around that realistic pace rather than an aspirational one.
