## The Resilience Argument Is Doing a Lot of Unexamined Work

"Resilience" has become the default justification for reshoring and nearshoring decisions across industrials manufacturing — a near-universal talking point in board discussions about supply chain strategy since the disruptions of the early 2020s. It's also, in our experience advising manufacturers through actual network reconfiguration decisions, a term doing more rhetorical work than analytical work in most of these conversations. Resilience is real and valuable, but it is not free, and the manufacturers making the best reshoring decisions are the ones quantifying the resilience benefit rigorously enough to weigh it honestly against the real cost premium reshoring usually carries — rather than treating "resilience" as a justification that ends the analysis rather than starting it.

## What Reshoring Actually Costs

The headline comparison — offshore unit cost versus onshore unit cost — understates the true economics in both directions, but manufacturers evaluating reshoring decisions most commonly underweight the onshore cost side. Labor cost differentials remain real and substantial in most reshoring comparisons, even accounting for automation investment that can offset some of the gap. Facility construction and ramp-up in a new location carries real capital cost and execution risk, particularly for manufacturers reshoring capability that's been offshore long enough that domestic supplier ecosystems and skilled-labor pools have atrophied — a cost that's easy to underestimate because it doesn't show up as a clean line item in a standard cost comparison.

## What Resilience Is Actually Worth — When You Quantify It

The manufacturers doing this analysis well aren't rejecting the resilience argument; they're insisting on quantifying it with the same rigor applied to the cost side, using a framework closer to the scenario planning methodology than a qualitative resilience narrative.

This means building explicit disruption scenarios — a major supplier facility outage, a trade-policy shift restricting a key input or export market, a significant transportation disruption — and quantifying the realistic financial exposure of the current network configuration under each scenario, including lost revenue from stockouts, expedited-shipping cost premiums during disruption, and customer relationship damage from service failures during a disruption event. That quantified exposure, not a qualitative sense that reshoring "feels safer," is the actual resilience benefit that should be weighed against reshoring's cost premium.

It also means recognizing that resilience is not a binary onshore-versus-offshore question — dual-sourcing strategies, strategic inventory buffers for critical inputs, and diversified (rather than fully reshored) supplier networks can capture a meaningful share of the resilience benefit at a fraction of full reshoring's cost premium, and for many manufacturers represent a better risk-adjusted answer than full reshoring once both sides of the ledger are quantified honestly.

## The Trade Policy Variable Nobody Can Fully Model

A genuine complication in this analysis, and one that's gotten harder rather than easier in recent years: trade policy is a source of uncertainty that doesn't behave like a normal business risk factor, and manufacturers building reshoring business cases need to treat it as a distinct scenario variable rather than folding it into a generic "supply chain disruption" category. Tariff and trade-policy scenarios can shift the reshoring economics dramatically and with limited warning, in either direction — sometimes strengthening the case for reshoring, sometimes (where reshored production still depends on imported inputs subject to their own tariff exposure) undermining it in ways that aren't obvious from a simple finished-goods cost comparison.

## What Good Practice Looks Like

The clearest pattern among manufacturers making reshoring decisions well: they treat the decision as a genuine quantitative network optimization problem — building explicit demand and disruption scenarios, quantifying resilience benefit rather than asserting it, and evaluating the full spectrum of options from full reshoring to targeted dual-sourcing — rather than a binary, sentiment-driven choice between "risky offshore" and "safe onshore." The honest finding, in most of the network optimization work we've done on this question, is that full reshoring is the right answer for a smaller share of product lines and facilities than the current strategic rhetoric suggests, and that a more surgical combination of selective reshoring, dual-sourcing, and strategic buffer inventory captures most of the quantified resilience benefit at meaningfully lower cost across the broader portfolio.
