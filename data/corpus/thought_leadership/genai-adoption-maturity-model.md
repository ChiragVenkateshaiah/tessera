## Most Enterprises Are Two Stages Below Where They Say They Are

Ask an executive team where they sit on their generative AI journey and the answer is usually "scaling." Ask what governance sits around the deployments, how value is measured, and how many of the tools have survived contact with a genuine production workflow, and the answer is usually consistent with something closer to structured experimentation.

This is not dishonesty. It is a measurement problem: adoption is easy to count and maturity is not. Seat licences, query volumes, and pilot counts all rise quickly, and all of them describe activity rather than capability. The five stages below are deliberately defined by governance, measurement, and workflow integration rather than by deployment count, because those are the things that determine whether the activity turns into durable value.

| Stage | Governance | Measurement | Scope | Characteristic failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Ad-hoc | None; individual initiative | None | Individuals using consumer tools | Shadow usage with no data controls |
| 2. Structured experimentation | Pilot approval process | Pilot-level qualitative feedback | Bounded pilots, several functions | Pilot portfolio with no exit criteria |
| 3. Governed deployment | Named accountable owner per use case | Usage and satisfaction metrics | Production in one or two functions | Usage mistaken for value |
| 4. Workflow-embedded | Inside existing model risk and control frameworks | Downstream business outcomes | Redesigned workflows, not augmented tasks | Measurement outruns attribution |
| 5. Capability | Standard part of change governance | Portfolio-level value tracking with retirement decisions | Enterprise-wide, with things deliberately switched off | Complacency; the capability stops being questioned |

### Stage 1 — Ad-hoc

Individuals adopt consumer tools without organisational involvement. Value is real but invisible and unmanaged, and so is the risk.

- No view of what data is leaving the organisation
- No consistency in how output is checked before use
- Genuine productivity gains that no one can point to or defend

The mistake here is treating this stage as something to eliminate rather than something to surface. Enterprises that respond with a blanket prohibition typically drive the same behaviour further underground; those that respond with a sanctioned tool and clear data rules convert it into stage two within a quarter.

### Stage 2 — Structured Experimentation

Pilots are approved, scoped, and run. This is where most organisations that describe themselves as "scaling" actually sit.

- A portfolio of pilots, often more than the organisation can properly evaluate
- Success defined as "the pilot worked," not as a business outcome
- No standing decision about what happens when a pilot ends

The defining weakness is the absence of exit criteria. A pilot without a pre-agreed threshold for proceeding, stopping, or extending does not conclude — it persists, quietly consuming attention. The single highest-return intervention at this stage is to write the exit criterion before the pilot starts, and to actually stop the pilots that miss it.

### Stage 3 — Governed Deployment

One or two use cases reach genuine production with a named owner and real controls.

- Accountability sits with a business owner, not the technology function
- Access, retention, and data handling rules are enforced rather than documented
- Metrics exist, but are still predominantly usage metrics

This is the stage where measurement becomes the binding constraint. Usage rates rise, the programme reports success, and no one can yet connect that activity to an outcome anyone outside the programme cares about. Organisations stall here for a long time, and the stall is rarely technical.

### Stage 4 — Workflow-Embedded

The workflow is redesigned around the capability rather than the capability being added to the existing workflow.

- The system sits inside the existing model risk, audit, and control perimeter
- Measurement traces through to downstream outcomes: cycle time, error rates, capacity reallocation
- Human accountability is explicitly designed rather than assumed

The distinction from stage three is genuine and often uncomfortable, because embedding means changing roles, review steps, and sometimes headcount assumptions. Organisations that stop at "we gave everyone a tool" have not reached this stage regardless of how high adoption is, because the work itself has not changed shape.

### Stage 5 — Capability

The organisation manages a portfolio, including deliberate retirement.

- New use cases go through standard change governance, not a special AI process
- Value tracking is portfolio-level, and unsuccessful deployments are switched off
- The organisation can say which use cases are not worth continuing, and has acted on it

The clearest signal of stage five is subtractive rather than additive. An organisation that has never retired a generative AI deployment either has an implausible hit rate or is not measuring honestly.

## What the Model Is For

The point of staging is not to rank organisations. It is to make the next move obvious, and the next move is almost always the governance and measurement gap of the current stage rather than another use case. An enterprise at stage two with fifteen pilots does not need a sixteenth; it needs exit criteria. An enterprise at stage three with strong usage numbers does not need broader rollout; it needs one credible outcome measurement that would survive a sceptical finance director.

The organisations moving fastest are conspicuously not the ones with the largest deployment counts. They are the ones that treated the discipline as the deliverable — the ones that could answer "how would we know if this stopped working" before they scaled anything — and that discipline, rather than any particular tool choice, is what has proven to transfer as the technology underneath keeps changing.
