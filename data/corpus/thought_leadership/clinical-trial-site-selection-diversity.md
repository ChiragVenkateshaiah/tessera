## Two Conversations That Should Be One

Clinical trial site selection has traditionally been optimized primarily for enrollment speed and cost — which sites can recruit eligible patients fastest, at what cost per patient, with what track record of data quality. Trial population diversity has, for most of the industry's history, been treated as a separate conversation, often framed primarily in regulatory or ethical terms rather than as a factor materially affecting a program's commercial and clinical success. We think this separation is increasingly a strategic mistake — the two conversations are converging, for reasons that go well beyond regulatory compliance.

## Why Diversity Is Becoming a Commercial, Not Just Ethical, Question

**Regulatory expectations have shifted from encouragement to increasingly explicit expectation.** Multiple major regulatory bodies have moved toward more formal expectations around trial population diversity and representativeness, including diversity action plans as part of trial design submissions in some jurisdictions. Programs designed around historical site-selection logic that treats diversity as a secondary consideration are increasingly running into friction at the regulatory review stage — a cost that shows up much later in the program than the original site-selection decision, which is precisely what makes it easy to underweight at the time that decision gets made.

**Real-world evidence generation increasingly depends on trial population representativeness.** As value-based contracting and real-world evidence become more central to pricing and market access strategy (see the value-based contracting piece), a trial population that doesn't reasonably represent the real-world patient population undermines the credibility of downstream real-world evidence claims — a manufacturer that optimized purely for enrollment speed in a narrow, non-representative population may find that data less useful for the market access conversations that increasingly determine commercial success post-approval.

**Underrepresented populations are, in many therapeutic areas, a growing share of the addressable patient population.** A trial program that systematically under-enrolls populations with meaningfully different treatment response or disease prevalence isn't just a representativeness problem in the abstract — it's a market-sizing and product-positioning risk, because clinical evidence gaps in specific populations become commercial gaps when it's time to make the case for the therapy to prescribers and payers serving those populations.

## What Better Site Selection Practice Looks Like

The manufacturers doing this well have moved site selection from a narrowly enrollment-speed-optimized decision to a genuinely multi-factor decision, incorporating population representativeness as a first-class criterion alongside speed and cost — not a compliance check applied after the primary site list is already set.

This means building site-selection criteria that explicitly account for the demographic and clinical profile of the population a site can realistically enroll, not just its historical enrollment velocity — sites with strong track records in fast, low-friction enrollment sometimes achieve that speed partly because they draw from a narrower, more homogeneous patient population than the disease's true real-world population, and that trade-off needs to be visible in the site-selection decision, not discovered after the trial is fully enrolled.

It also means earlier, more deliberate investment in site relationships and infrastructure in settings — community health centers, sites serving specific underrepresented populations — that may have real barriers to fast enrollment (less trial infrastructure, less research-coordinator capacity) but are essential to a representative trial population. This is a genuine trade-off against pure enrollment speed, and the manufacturers managing it well are building the business case for that trade-off explicitly — using the market-sizing and value-based pricing discipline the rest of their commercial organization already applies elsewhere — rather than treating diversity investment as a cost with no quantifiable return.

## The Strategic Reframe

The manufacturers still treating trial diversity purely as a regulatory or ethical obligation, separate from commercial strategy, are increasingly missing the more important point: representativeness is becoming a direct input into whether the resulting clinical and real-world evidence actually supports the commercial and market access case the therapy needs to make, in front of increasingly diverse patient populations and increasingly outcomes-focused payers. Treating site selection and diversity strategy as one integrated decision — rather than two separate conversations running in parallel — is a genuine competitive advantage for the manufacturers getting there first, both in regulatory smoothness and in the durability of the commercial case the resulting evidence can support.
