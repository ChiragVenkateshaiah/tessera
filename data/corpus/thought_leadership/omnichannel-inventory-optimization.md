## The Reset Nobody Fully Absorbed

The pandemic forced retailers into a crash course in omnichannel fulfillment — curbside pickup, ship-from-store, buy-online-pickup-in-store all went from marginal capability to operational necessity within months. Several years on, most retailers have kept the channels but never fully re-optimized the underlying inventory logic that was built for a single-channel world and then patched, rather than redesigned, to support omnichannel demand. The result is a quiet but persistent tax on margin: inventory that's frequently in the wrong location relative to where omnichannel demand actually needs it, propped up by manual workarounds store and fulfillment teams have built to compensate.

## Why the Old Inventory Logic Doesn't Transfer

Traditional retail inventory allocation was built around a simple, largely accurate assumption: a store's inventory need is a function of that store's own point-of-sale demand. Omnichannel breaks this assumption in a specific and under-appreciated way — a store's true inventory need now depends on demand from customers who will never physically visit that store, routed there because it's the nearest fulfillment point for an online order. Allocation models still anchored to point-of-sale-only demand systematically under-stock the SKUs and locations that omnichannel fulfillment actually depends on, while over-stocking based on a demand signal that's become an incomplete picture of true consumption.

## What Genuine Re-Optimization Requires

**Unified demand visibility across channels, at the SKU-location level.** This sounds obvious and is harder than it sounds — most retailers' online and in-store demand data still live in genuinely separate systems with imperfect reconciliation, which means the "true" demand signal for allocation decisions is often an approximation built on incomplete data, not the real thing.

**Allocation logic that treats a store's fulfillment role explicitly, not implicitly.** A store that serves meaningfully as a fulfillment node for online orders in its catchment area has a different true inventory need than an otherwise-identical store that doesn't — the allocation model needs a variable for fulfillment role, not just historical POS velocity, or it will keep under-stocking the stores doing the most fulfillment work.

**Dynamic reallocation, not a static seasonal plan.** Omnichannel demand patterns — which channels drive which locations' fulfillment burden — shift faster than the seasonal planning cycles most inventory systems were built around. Retailers seeing the strongest omnichannel inventory performance have moved toward more frequent, closer-to-real-time reallocation logic rather than a quarterly or seasonal static plan.

**Honest measurement of the fulfillment cost trade-off.** Ship-from-store and similar fulfillment models solve an inventory-visibility problem but introduce real incremental cost (store labor time diverted to fulfillment, higher per-unit shipping cost than a dedicated fulfillment center). Retailers that measure and optimize purely for inventory efficiency, without weighing the true fulfillment cost, can end up with a network that looks efficient on an inventory dashboard and erodes margin in ways that don't show up in the same report.

## The Uncomfortable Organizational Implication

The technical fix — better demand data, better allocation models — is necessary but not sufficient. In most of the retail organizations we've worked with, inventory planning, store operations, and e-commerce fulfillment still sit in largely separate organizational silos, each optimizing its own metrics without a shared view of total omnichannel cost and service performance. The retailers making the most progress have paired the technical re-optimization work with a genuine operating model change — usually some version of a unified demand-and-fulfillment planning function that has visibility and accountability across what used to be three separate organizational silos. Without that structural change, better allocation algorithms tend to get implemented against the same fragmented organizational logic that created the original mismatch, and the improvement plateaus well short of what the technology alone could deliver.

## Where the Margin Actually Is

Retailers who've done this work seriously report the gains showing up less in headline inventory turns and more in a quieter, harder-to-headline metric: reduced markdown rate on omnichannel-influenced SKUs, because inventory is more often in a location that can actually serve the demand that exists for it, rather than sitting in a store waiting for local foot traffic that omnichannel shopping behavior has already partially replaced. It's not a flashy transformation story, but for retailers running on thin margins, it's one of the more durable ones available.
