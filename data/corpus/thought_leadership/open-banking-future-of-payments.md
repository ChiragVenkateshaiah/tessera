## A Slower Revolution Than Advertised, But a Revolution Nonetheless

Open banking arrived with predictions of rapid, sweeping disruption to incumbent payment rails — and then, in most markets, proceeded much more slowly than forecast. That slower pace has led some incumbent bank executives to conclude the threat was overstated. We think that conclusion is wrong, and dangerously so. Open banking's restructuring of payments is real; it has simply moved at the pace of infrastructure and trust-building rather than the pace of a typical fintech product launch, and the institutions that mistake slow for stalled are going to be caught flat-footed as the curve steepens.

## What Actually Changed Under the Surface

The headline open banking use case — a customer authorizing a third party to view their account data — was never the interesting part. The interesting part is what account-to-account (A2A) payment initiation, built on the same regulatory and technical infrastructure, does to the economics of the payment stack.

Card-based payments carry interchange economics that have shaped retail and e-commerce business models for decades — a cost merchants have largely had no way to avoid for card-present or card-not-present transactions. A2A payments initiated directly from a customer's bank account, authenticated through open banking rails, bypass that interchange structure entirely. For a large merchant processing significant payment volume, the economics of even partial migration from card to A2A are material enough to justify real investment in customer experience to drive adoption — and several large retailers and platforms have quietly begun doing exactly that, offering incentives for customers who pay via account-linked methods rather than card.

This is the pattern that should worry incumbent card issuers and acquirers more than any individual fintech competitor: the economic incentive to shift volume away from card rails doesn't depend on any single company's success. It's a structural pull that persists regardless of which specific fintech wins any given market, because the underlying economics favor the merchant side of the transaction.

## Three Predictions for the Next Phase

**Variable recurring payments (VRP) become the wedge for subscription and bill-pay migration.** Fixed direct debit has existed for decades; what open banking infrastructure adds is variable, API-authorized recurring payment with tighter, more flexible consent controls. Subscription businesses and bill-pay use cases — high volume, recurring, currently mostly card- or direct-debit-based — are a natural next migration target once VRP infrastructure matures across major markets, because the cost savings compound with transaction frequency.

**Embedded finance accelerates the shift, not slows it.** As more non-financial platforms embed payment initiation directly into their own customer experience (a marketplace, a SaaS platform, a logistics provider), the payment method choice increasingly gets made by the platform, not the individual consumer at checkout — and platforms have a direct economic incentive to default customers toward lower-cost rails. This shifts the locus of the "card vs. A2A" decision away from consumer habit (which changes slowly) toward platform economics (which can shift faster).

**Regulatory divergence creates real strategic complexity, not just compliance overhead.** Open banking regulatory frameworks are diverging across major markets rather than converging on a single global standard — different consent models, different data-sharing scope, different payment initiation rules. For any financial institution or fintech operating across multiple markets, this isn't a compliance footnote; it materially shapes which product features and go-to-market approaches transfer across borders and which have to be rebuilt market by market.

## What This Means for Incumbent Strategy

The instinct among some incumbent banks has been to treat open banking primarily as a defensive compliance obligation — build the minimum required API surface, satisfy the regulator, move on. That posture cedes the more interesting strategic question: banks sit on the underlying account infrastructure that open banking payment initiation depends on, and are well positioned to build their own A2A payment products rather than simply enabling third parties to disintermediate them. The institutions treating open banking as a build-vs-defend strategic choice — rather than a compliance checkbox — are the ones most likely to capture rather than cede the payments-economics shift now underway. The infrastructure is already built; the strategic question is who uses it to build the next generation of payment products, and that window is still genuinely open.
