## Ambition Isn't the Constraint Anymore

Heavy manufacturing decarbonization commitments have become close to table stakes across the industrials sector — most major manufacturers now have public emissions-reduction targets on a defined timeline. The constraint that actually determines whether those commitments get met is not ambition, and increasingly not even technology availability in the abstract. It's sequencing: which decarbonization levers to pursue in which order, given that most heavy manufacturing facilities can't practically pursue every available lever simultaneously without disrupting production, and capital is genuinely constrained even at manufacturers publicly committed to the transition.

## The Lever Menu Is Not Interchangeable

Heavy manufacturing decarbonization levers fall into categories with very different cost, disruption, and abatement-potential profiles, and treating them as an interchangeable menu to select from based on abatement cost alone — the standard marginal abatement cost curve approach — misses important practical constraints that a purely cost-ranked list doesn't capture.

**Energy efficiency and process optimization** typically offers the fastest payback and lowest disruption, but a genuinely limited total abatement ceiling — it reduces emissions intensity but rarely gets an already-efficient facility close to its long-term target on its own.

**Electrification of process heat**, where technically feasible for the specific process, offers substantial abatement potential but usually requires significant capital investment and, critically, depends on the carbon intensity of the local grid — electrification in a region with a still-carbon-intensive grid captures much less real abatement benefit than the same investment in a region with cleaner grid power, a factor that should shape where in a global manufacturing footprint electrification investment gets prioritized first.

**Alternative fuels and feedstocks** (green hydrogen, biomass, and others depending on process) often carry the highest abatement potential for genuinely hard-to-electrify high-heat processes, but currently carry real cost premiums and, in many cases, supply availability constraints that make them a later-stage rather than immediate lever for most facilities.

**Carbon capture** remains, for most heavy manufacturing applications, the most capital-intensive and highest-uncertainty lever — appropriate consideration for specific hard-to-abate process emissions where no viable substitution exists, but a poor first move relative to lower-cost, lower-risk levers still available at most facilities.

## Why Sequencing, Not Just Selection, Determines Success

The manufacturers we've seen make the most credible progress against public decarbonization targets share a common practice: they've built an explicit, facility-by-facility sequencing plan — not just a portfolio-level target and a menu of technologies — that accounts for capital availability constraints, production disruption tolerance, and the genuine technology-readiness timeline for each lever at each specific facility, rather than assuming every facility can move on every available lever simultaneously.

This matters because the naive approach — pursuing the theoretically lowest-marginal-abatement-cost lever first across the entire portfolio — frequently produces a sequencing plan that's technically optimal on paper and practically undeliverable, because it doesn't account for capital constraints that mean facilities are effectively competing with each other for the same limited transition capital budget, or for production disruption tolerance that varies significantly by facility depending on current utilization and customer commitments.

## The Capital Allocation Question Boards Are Still Avoiding

The harder and less comfortable version of the sequencing question is a capital allocation one: given genuinely constrained transition capital, which facilities and which levers get funded first, and on what basis — lowest abatement cost per dollar, highest absolute abatement, strategic importance of the facility to the broader business, or some deliberately weighted combination? We've observed that many manufacturers have avoided making this trade-off explicit, instead funding decarbonization initiatives more opportunistically as individual business cases clear a hurdle rate, without a portfolio-level view of whether the resulting sequence is actually the best achievable path to the public target given the real capital constraint.

## What Better Practice Requires

Treating heavy manufacturing decarbonization as a genuine capital allocation and sequencing problem — using the same rigor a strategic capital allocation decision would get elsewhere in the business, including honest scenario planning around technology cost curves and regulatory trajectory — rather than as a sustainability-function-owned target-setting exercise disconnected from the core capital planning process, is the difference we're seeing between manufacturers on a credible path to their public commitments and manufacturers whose targets are increasingly at risk of becoming aspirational rather than achieved.
