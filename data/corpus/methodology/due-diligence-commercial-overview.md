## Overview

Commercial due diligence assesses whether an acquisition target's market position, growth prospects, and competitive standing justify the price being paid — the "is this a good business" question that sits alongside (and is distinct from) financial due diligence's "are the numbers real" question. It's typically the workstream that most directly informs the deal's investment thesis.

## When to Use It

Use commercial due diligence during any acquisition process, buy-side or sell-side, once a target has been identified and the deal has progressed past initial screening into a structured diligence phase.

## Framework

1. **Validate the market.** Assess the size, growth rate, and structural attractiveness of the target's market — using the same market sizing and competitive landscape techniques used in market entry analysis, applied here to validate rather than discover the opportunity.
2. **Assess the target's competitive position.** Is the target's market share, growth rate, and profitability trajectory improving, stable, or eroding relative to competitors — and why? A target with strong historical financials but eroding competitive position is a materially different (and riskier) investment than one with genuine, durable competitive advantage.
3. **Stress-test the growth plan.** Management's forward projections are almost always optimistic. Independently assess whether the growth assumptions (market growth, share gain, pricing) are achievable given the competitive dynamics validated in step 2.
4. **Identify red flags for escalation.** Any finding materially inconsistent with the deal thesis — a market growing more slowly than assumed, a key customer concentration risk, an emerging competitive threat — needs to be flagged clearly and early (see the red flag reporting methodology page), not buried in a comprehensive report the deal team may not read in full before signing.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Confirming the thesis rather than testing it.** Commercial due diligence teams under deal-timeline pressure can drift toward validating management's story rather than independently stress-testing it — the value of the workstream depends on genuine independence.
- **Generic market analysis disconnected from the specific deal thesis.** A thorough market sizing exercise that doesn't connect back to the specific assumptions underpinning the valuation isn't actionable for the deal team.
- **Late-surfacing red flags.** Findings that would change the investment decision need to reach the deal team as soon as they're identified, not held for the final report — by the time a comprehensive report lands, the deal timeline may not allow for renegotiation.
