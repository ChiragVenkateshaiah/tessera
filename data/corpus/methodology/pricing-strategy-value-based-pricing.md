## Overview

Value-based pricing sets price as a function of the quantified value a product delivers to the customer, rather than internal cost or competitor price points. It captures the most margin of the three pricing logics but requires the most analytical work to defend, because it depends on a credible, quantified value story — not just an assertion that the product is "better."

## When to Use It

Use value-based pricing where a genuinely differentiated value proposition exists and can be quantified in terms the customer's own economics recognize — clinical outcomes and total cost of care in pharma and medtech, productivity or cost-avoidance in enterprise software and industrial equipment. It is a poor fit in commoditized categories where differentiation is minimal or unprovable.

## Framework

1. **Build the value model.** Quantify the customer-side impact of the product relative to the next-best alternative (including "do nothing"), in the customer's own units — cost per outcome, total cost of ownership, productivity gain. This model needs to survive scrutiny from a sophisticated buyer, not just sound plausible internally.
2. **Determine value share.** Price is typically set to capture a portion of the quantified value delivered, leaving the customer with a visible net benefit versus the alternative — full value capture rarely survives negotiation or competitive pressure.
3. **Validate with the customer, not just internally.** Test the value story directly with payers or customers before finalizing price — internal conviction about a product's value is not evidence that the buyer will recognize or pay for it.
4. **Build the access/adoption case alongside the price.** For categories with a formal reimbursement or procurement gate (health systems, government buyers), the value dossier needs to be built for the specific evidentiary standard that gate requires, not a generic value narrative.

**Worked example.** A specialty pharma manufacturer preparing to launch a new therapy for a chronic condition initially proposed pricing anchored to the cost of existing standard-of-care drugs plus a modest premium — a cost-plus-adjacent logic dressed up as value pricing. Rebuilding the value model around total cost of care (avoided hospitalizations, reduced need for adjunct therapies, quantified using real-world evidence from comparable markets) supported a materially higher price point, provided the health-economic dossier could withstand payer scrutiny — which required a level of clinical and economic evidence generation that had to be planned well before launch, not assembled after the pricing decision was made.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Value stories that don't survive buyer scrutiny.** An internally compelling value narrative that isn't quantified in the buyer's own economic terms, or isn't backed by evidence the buyer's process accepts, collapses in negotiation.
- **Full value capture.** Pricing to capture 100% of quantified value leaves the customer with no visible reason to switch or adopt — some value share needs to remain with the buyer.
- **Treating value-based pricing as a one-time exercise.** Value models need updating as competitive alternatives, evidence base, and buyer economics evolve — a value price set at launch can become stale within 2-3 years.
