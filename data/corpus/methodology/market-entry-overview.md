## Overview

Market entry analysis answers a single question for a client considering a new geography, segment, or adjacency: should we go in, and if so, how? It is one of the most frequently requested engagements at Meridian, and one of the easiest to do badly — teams that skip straight to a market-sizing spreadsheet without first framing the decision produce numbers nobody trusts.

This page is the entry point into the market entry toolkit. It links out to the three companion methodologies: market sizing, competitive landscape mapping, and entry mode selection. Read this first before using any of them.

## When to Use It

Use this framework when a client is evaluating whether to enter a new country, launch into an adjacent customer segment, or extend an existing product line into a market they don't currently serve. It is not the right tool for pricing decisions inside an existing market (see the pricing strategy methodology) or for pure cost questions (see cost transformation).

## Framework

A market entry analysis has four stages, each with its own deliverable:

1. **Opportunity framing.** Before sizing anything, agree with the client on what "success" looks like — revenue threshold, market share target, strategic rationale (e.g., defensive move against a competitor, or a genuine growth bet). Document this explicitly; it prevents scope creep later when the numbers come back ambiguous.
2. **Market sizing.** Build TAM/SAM/SOM using the market-sizing methodology page. Triangulate top-down and bottom-up estimates — a single-method estimate is not defensible in a partner review.
3. **Competitive landscape mapping.** Identify incumbents, their relative strength, and white space. This stage usually reveals whether the "opportunity" the client sees is actually contested ground.
4. **Entry mode and go/no-go recommendation.** Combine the sizing and competitive read with the client's risk appetite and capability gaps to recommend an entry mode (organic build, partnership/JV, or acquisition) or a clean no-go.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Sizing before framing.** Analysts default to building a TAM model in the first week because it feels like progress. Without an agreed success bar, the number has no decision attached to it.
- **Ignoring capability gaps.** A market can be large and attractive and still be a bad entry for a specific client if they lack distribution, regulatory relationships, or brand recognition required to compete.
- **Treating go/no-go as binary too early.** Most engagements land on a conditional recommendation (enter via partnership, enter a sub-segment first, wait 18 months) rather than a clean yes/no. Don't force false precision.
