## Overview

Competitive pricing response frameworks help a client decide how — or whether — to react when a competitor moves price. The instinctive reaction (match immediately) is frequently the wrong one; a disciplined framework separates moves that require a response from moves that don't, and shapes the response to protect margin rather than just defend share reflexively.

## When to Use It

Use this whenever a client faces (or is considering initiating) a competitive price move — a competitor cutting price to gain share, a new low-cost entrant, or the client's own team asking whether to pre-emptively move price ahead of an anticipated competitor action.

## Framework

1. **Classify the competitor move.** Is it a genuine strategic repositioning (durable, likely to persist), a tactical/promotional move (temporary), or a distress signal (a competitor with cost or capital problems)? Each calls for a different response, and misreading a tactical move as strategic (or vice versa) leads to overreaction or underreaction.
2. **Assess exposure.** Quantify which customer segments are actually at risk from the competitor's move — not all customers are equally price-sensitive or equally exposed to the competitor in question. A blanket price match protects segments that were never at risk while giving away margin unnecessarily.
3. **Choose a response type.** Options beyond direct price matching include: selective/targeted matching (only in the exposed segments), value-add response (bundle, service, loyalty enhancement instead of price), or deliberate non-response (accepting some share loss in a low-value segment rather than triggering a margin-destructive price war).
4. **War-game the second-order response.** Any response the client makes may itself trigger a further competitor reaction. Model at least one round of back-and-forth before committing, particularly in concentrated markets with a small number of rational competitors.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Reflexive full price-matching.** The default instinct, and usually the most margin-destructive option — it treats all exposure as equal and escalates competitive intensity industry-wide.
- **Misclassifying a strategic move as tactical.** Treating a genuine repositioning (e.g., a competitor's structural cost advantage from a new operating model) as a temporary promotion, and waiting it out, cedes share the client won't easily win back.
- **No second-order thinking.** Failing to anticipate how a competitor might respond to your response turns a single pricing decision into an unplanned, margin-eroding price war.
