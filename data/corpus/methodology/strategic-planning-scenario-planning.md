## Overview

Scenario planning builds a small number of internally consistent, materially different future states of a client's operating environment, and stress-tests strategic choices against all of them — a deliberate alternative to single-point-forecast planning, which tends to understate uncertainty and leave organizations exposed when reality diverges from the base case.

## When to Use It

Use when a client faces genuine structural uncertainty over their planning horizon — regulatory change, technology disruption, geopolitical or trade-policy shifts, commodity price volatility — rather than routine, forecastable variation. Particularly relevant for industrials and other capital-intensive clients making long-lived, hard-to-reverse investment decisions (a new manufacturing facility, a multi-year supply chain reconfiguration) under real uncertainty.

## Framework

1. **Identify the critical uncertainties.** Not every uncertain factor deserves scenario treatment — focus on the two or three factors that are both highly uncertain and highly impactful on the strategic decision at hand (e.g., for an industrials client: trade policy direction and energy cost trajectory).
2. **Build a small number of distinct, internally consistent scenarios** — typically three to four, built around combinations of the critical uncertainties, not as a simple optimistic/pessimistic/base-case spread along a single dimension. Each scenario should tell a coherent story of how the world got there, not just specify different numbers.
3. **Stress-test the strategic option under consideration against every scenario.** A strategy that only performs well in one scenario is a bet, not a resilient strategy — the goal is to find choices that perform acceptably across the plausible range, or to identify which scenario the client is implicitly betting on if they proceed anyway.
4. **Identify early warning indicators.** For each scenario, define observable leading indicators that would signal the world is moving toward that scenario — this turns scenario planning from a one-time exercise into an ongoing monitoring discipline the client can actually use to adapt in real time.

**Worked example.** An industrials manufacturer evaluating a major new production facility investment faced genuine uncertainty on two fronts: trade policy affecting input costs and export markets, and the pace of a shift toward domestic reshoring among its own customer base. Building four scenarios around combinations of these two uncertainties (rather than a single "optimistic/pessimistic" spread) revealed that the proposed facility location performed well in three of the four scenarios but was significantly exposed in a fourth — prompting a redesign of the facility's flexibility (dual-sourcing capability for a key input) that reduced downside exposure in the fourth scenario at a modest incremental cost, a change the original single-forecast plan would never have surfaced.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Optimistic/pessimistic spread on a single variable.** Produces scenarios that are just quantitatively different versions of the same story, not genuinely distinct futures — misses the value of true scenario diversity.
- **Too many scenarios.** More than four or so scenarios becomes unwieldy to plan against in practice and dilutes decision-maker attention rather than sharpening it.
- **No monitoring discipline after the exercise.** Scenario planning delivered as a one-time report, without ongoing tracking of the early warning indicators, loses its value as the actual future unfolds.
