## Overview

Cost transformation engagements aim to durably improve a client's cost structure — not a one-off cut, but a reset that holds after the consulting team leaves. The distinction matters: clients that treat cost transformation as a single budget-cutting exercise usually see costs creep back within 18 months. This page frames the overall approach; the companion pages (zero-based budgeting, SG&A benchmarking, procurement cost reduction) cover specific techniques.

## When to Use It

Use this framework when a client's core ask is margin improvement, not top-line growth — typically triggered by margin compression from competitive pricing pressure, a private equity ownership change, or a need to fund a strategic investment by freeing up cash internally.

## Framework

1. **Baseline and benchmark.** Establish the current cost structure by category (COGS, SG&A, procurement spend) and benchmark against comparable peers. Without an external benchmark, "high" or "low" cost is just an opinion.
2. **Identify the lever mix.** Cost transformation levers generally fall into three buckets: structural (organization redesign, shared services), process (automation, procurement renegotiation), and zero-based reset (rebuilding the budget from a blank sheet rather than trimming last year's number). Most engagements use a combination.
3. **Size and sequence.** Quantify each lever's savings potential and implementation difficulty, then sequence — quick wins first to build credibility and self-fund harder structural moves.
4. **Build the case for change and governance.** Cost transformation fails on execution more often than on analysis. A tracked savings ledger with named owners per initiative is what keeps a program alive past the diagnostic phase.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Analysis without a delivery plan.** A beautifully sized savings opportunity that never gets a named owner and a tracked milestone plan does not become realized savings.
- **Cutting without a floor.** Aggressive cost-outs that damage the capability needed to serve customers create a bounce-back problem — costs return, often with a service-quality hit that's harder to fix than the original cost problem.
- **No baseline benchmark.** Without comparing to peers, "aggressive" savings targets are arbitrary and hard to defend internally.
