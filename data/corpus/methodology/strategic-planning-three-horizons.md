## Overview

The three-horizons framework organizes a client's strategic initiatives and investment portfolio into three time-and-risk categories — defending and extending the core business (Horizon 1), building emerging opportunities (Horizon 2), and seeding options for longer-term, higher-uncertainty bets (Horizon 3). Its main value is forcing an explicit conversation about portfolio balance, since organizations left to their own devices systematically over-invest in Horizon 1.

## When to Use It

Use during strategic planning cycles, particularly for clients whose growth has stalled or whose leadership senses the current business, however healthy today, faces a structural threat within a 3-7 year horizon and needs a deliberate framework for balancing near-term performance against future-proofing investment.

## Framework

1. **Categorize existing initiatives and investments.** Horizon 1: initiatives that defend and extend current core business performance, generally lower risk, nearer-term payback. Horizon 2: emerging opportunities — adjacent markets, new business models — with a 2-5 year horizon to material scale, higher uncertainty. Horizon 3: options and bets on a 5+ year horizon, high uncertainty, often exploratory in nature.
2. **Assess current portfolio balance.** Most organizations, left unmanaged, find that 90%+ of investment and leadership attention sits in Horizon 1 — not necessarily wrong, but frequently a default rather than a deliberate choice. Make the current allocation explicit before debating whether to change it.
3. **Set a deliberate target allocation.** The right balance varies by industry clock-speed and the client's specific competitive threat profile — a client in a slow-moving, stable industry can reasonably run a Horizon-1-heavy portfolio; a client facing fast-moving disruption risk needs materially more in Horizons 2 and 3, even at the cost of near-term margin.
4. **Apply different governance to each horizon.** Horizon 1 initiatives should be governed with standard ROI and payback discipline; Horizon 3 initiatives need different metrics (learning velocity, option value) since normal ROI discipline will kill legitimate long-horizon bets before they've had a chance to prove out.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Applying Horizon 1 governance to Horizon 3 bets.** Judging an exploratory Horizon 3 initiative against near-term ROI criteria designed for Horizon 1 kills it prematurely — a frequent, quiet way strategic ambition gets strangled by standard planning process.
- **Categorizing by wishful thinking rather than honest assessment.** Initiatives get mislabeled as "Horizon 2" because Horizon 3 sounds too speculative to defend in a budget review, distorting the whole portfolio view.
- **No explicit rebalancing decision.** Building the three-horizons view without translating it into an actual reallocation of budget and leadership attention leaves the exercise as analysis without consequence.

## Related Frameworks

Scenario planning (a complementary technique for stress-testing Horizon 2/3 bets under uncertainty), market entry analysis (often the vehicle through which Horizon 2 opportunities get pursued).
