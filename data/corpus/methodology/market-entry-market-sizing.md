## Overview

TAM/SAM/SOM (Total Addressable Market, Serviceable Addressable Market, Serviceable Obtainable Market) is the standard nested-funnel approach to sizing an opportunity. It is a simple idea that is routinely executed badly, usually because teams don't triangulate and don't state their assumptions where a partner can stress-test them.

## When to Use It

Any time a client asks "how big is this market" — as a standalone question, or as stage 2 of the broader market entry analysis framework.

## Framework

**Define the funnel.**
- *TAM* — the total demand for the product/service category, assuming no constraints on the client's ability to capture it.
- *SAM* — the portion of TAM the client could realistically serve given geography, regulatory scope, and product fit.
- *SOM* — the portion of SAM the client can realistically capture given competitive intensity, go-to-market capability, and time horizon.

**Build two independent estimates.**
- *Top-down*: start from a published industry figure (analyst report, government statistics, trade body data) and narrow it using filtering logic (e.g., % of spend addressable by this product category, % of the population in the target segment).
- *Bottom-up*: build from unit economics — number of potential customers × expected penetration rate × average revenue per customer. This is slower but far more defensible, because every input is inspectable.

**Triangulate.** If top-down and bottom-up estimates diverge by more than roughly 30%, that gap is itself a finding — it usually means an assumption in one method is wrong, and finding out which one is more valuable than averaging the two numbers together.

**Sensitize.** Present a range, not a point estimate. Show how SOM moves under conservative, base, and aggressive penetration assumptions, and be explicit about which assumption drives the most variance — that's usually where client and team attention should focus.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Single-method sizing.** A bottom-up-only or top-down-only estimate without a cross-check is not defensible in front of a skeptical client sponsor.
- **False precision.** Presenting a SOM figure to the nearest million dollars implies a level of confidence the underlying assumptions don't support. Round appropriately and show the range.
- **Static sizing.** Markets move. A sizing exercise done at time T should note the CAGR assumption used to project forward, and flag if the underlying category is structurally growing, shrinking, or being disrupted.
- **Ignoring the SAM-to-SOM capability gap.** SAM tells you what's addressable in theory; SOM has to account for the client's actual go-to-market muscle, which is often the real constraint, not market size.
