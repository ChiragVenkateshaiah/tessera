## Overview

SG&A benchmarking compares a client's overhead cost structure against a defensible peer set, expressed as a ratio (typically SG&A as % of revenue, or cost-to-income ratio for financial services clients) to establish whether "high cost" is a fact or an assumption. It's the diagnostic step that should precede any cost-reduction target-setting.

## When to Use It

Early in any cost transformation engagement, before committing to a savings target. Also useful standalone when a client's board is asking "are we too expensive relative to peers" without a specific transformation program yet underway.

## Framework

1. **Select a defensible peer set.** Peers should match on business mix, geography, and scale — comparing a full-service regional bank to a monoline digital challenger produces a meaningless ratio. Where public peer data isn't available, use published industry cost-to-income benchmarks and clearly flag any assumptions made to normalize for mix differences.
2. **Normalize the comparison.** Adjust for one-off items (restructuring charges, litigation costs) on both sides so the comparison reflects run-rate cost structure, not accounting noise from a single year.
3. **Decompose the gap.** If the client's cost-to-income ratio sits above peer median, break the gap down by function (technology, operations, corporate/support) rather than reporting a single headline number — this is what turns a benchmark into an actionable target.
4. **Set a target with a credible path, not just a number.** A target cost-to-income ratio without a corresponding lever plan (see cost transformation overview) is just an aspiration.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Comparing against an unrepresentative peer set.** The single most common way this analysis loses credibility with a client's finance team — peer selection needs to survive scrutiny from people who know the industry cold.
- **Ignoring business mix differences.** A bank with a large wealth management arm will structurally carry different SG&A ratios than a pure retail lender; failing to adjust for mix produces a misleading gap.
- **Treating the benchmark as the target.** Matching peer median isn't automatically the right goal — a client with a genuinely differentiated service model may justify running above benchmark, and that's a legitimate strategic choice, not a failure to hit the number.
