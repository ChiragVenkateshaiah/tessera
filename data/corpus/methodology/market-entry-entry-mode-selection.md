## Overview

Once sizing and competitive mapping are done, the client still has to decide *how* to enter: build organically (greenfield), partner (joint venture or minority stake), or buy (acquisition). Each mode trades off speed, control, capital intensity, and risk very differently, and the "right" answer is rarely obvious from the sizing work alone.

## When to Use It

The final stage of the market entry framework, once you have a sized opportunity and a competitive read. Also useful as a standalone framework when a client has already committed to entering a market and just needs help choosing the mode.

## Framework

**Score the three modes against the client's actual constraints**, not in the abstract:

- **Greenfield (organic build)** — highest control, slowest speed to scale, requires the client to build local capability (regulatory licenses, distribution, brand) from zero. Best when the client has time, capital, and a genuinely differentiated model worth protecting from a partner.
- **Joint venture / partnership** — faster access to local capability (an existing bank's branch network and regulatory license, for instance), shared risk, but shared control and margin. Best when local capability is the binding constraint and a credible partner exists.
- **Acquisition** — fastest route to scale and existing customer relationships, highest capital outlay and integration risk. Best when speed matters more than cost, and a suitable target exists at a defensible price.

**Worked example.** A regional bank evaluating entry into a nearby market found that greenfield licensing alone would take 18-24 months before the first branch could open — too slow against the client's growth targets. A joint venture with a local financial institution that already held a banking license cut time-to-market to under six months, at the cost of a minority economic share of the new entity. An acquisition route existed in principle but at a premium that made the payback period longer than the JV route. The recommendation was JV with a pre-agreed buyout option several years out — a structure that let the client de-risk the entry decision without foreclosing full ownership later.

**Stress-test against the client's actual capability gaps**, identified during competitive mapping. If the constraint is regulatory access, a JV or acquisition that comes with an existing license solves it directly; greenfield does not.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Defaulting to acquisition because it's fastest.** Speed matters, but integration risk and price discipline matter more over a 3-5 year horizon — plenty of "fast" acquisitions destroy value on the integration side.
- **Ignoring exit/buyout terms in JV structures.** A JV without a pre-negotiated path to full ownership (or exit) leaves the client permanently dependent on a partner relationship that may sour.
- **Treating entry mode as separable from the earlier sizing/competitive work.** The right mode is a direct function of what the competitive mapping revealed about the binding constraint (capital, capability, or speed) — don't select a mode in isolation from that analysis.
