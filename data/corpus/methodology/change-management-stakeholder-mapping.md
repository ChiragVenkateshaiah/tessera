## Overview

Stakeholder mapping identifies who is affected by a change program, how much influence each stakeholder has over its success, and where they currently sit on support versus resistance — the foundational diagnostic for any change management workstream, whether attached to an operating model redesign, cost transformation, or M&A integration.

## When to Use It

Use at the start of any engagement involving organizational change significant enough to generate resistance — reorganizations, cost programs affecting headcount, system implementations, or post-merger integration. Revisit periodically through the program as stakeholder positions shift.

## Framework

1. **Identify the full stakeholder set.** Go beyond the obvious senior sponsors — include middle management (who often have the most influence over whether change actually takes hold day-to-day) and informal influencers who may not hold formal authority but shape peer sentiment.
2. **Plot on an influence/support grid.** Map each stakeholder on two axes: influence over the program's success, and current position (supportive, neutral, resistant). This produces four quadrants, each requiring a different engagement approach — high-influence resistors need direct, early engagement; low-influence resistors may not warrant the same investment of scarce change-management time.
3. **Diagnose the source of resistance, not just its presence.** Resistance is rarely irrational — it usually traces to a specific concern (loss of status, genuine belief the change is wrong, past experience with failed change programs). Understanding the specific driver shapes which engagement tactic will actually work.
4. **Build a differentiated engagement plan.** High-influence stakeholders (regardless of current position) warrant direct, frequent engagement; the plan should specify who engages whom, how often, and with what message — a generic communication plan aimed at "everyone" under-serves the stakeholders who most determine program success.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Mapping once and never updating.** Stakeholder positions shift as a program progresses — a map built at kickoff and never revisited misses both newly emerged resistance and stakeholders who've moved to support and could become active advocates.
- **Treating all resistance as equal.** Spending equal energy on a low-influence skeptic and a high-influence blocker misallocates scarce change-management effort.
- **Skipping the "why" behind resistance.** Generic reassurance messaging aimed at resistance whose actual source hasn't been diagnosed usually fails to shift the underlying concern.
